The problem

A CPG client (a major US retailer) planned trade promotions off out-of-stock forecasts that were too coarse to trust at the SKU × store level, so promo spend and safety stock were routinely mis-sized. The business needed accurate, per-series demand forecasts across a very large portfolio — 300,000+ SKU × store series over a 2TB dataset — refreshed with automated backtesting and hyperparameter tuning, fast enough to plan against.

Getting the promotion weeks right (the yellow band) is where the savings came from — that’s where a generic forecast fails and money is left on the table.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Training a separate model per series (300k+ of them, over a 2TB dataset) was a deliberate choice over one global model: it captured per-SKU, per-store seasonality and promo response that a pooled model washed out, at the cost of a much heavier training and orchestration burden. I ran this on a distributed stack (PySpark / Databricks / Dask) with automated backtesting and hyperparameter tuning per series, so the whole portfolio could retrain on 100M+ rows a day. The failure mode was under-forecasting: a coarser, less granular forecast doesn’t just miss on average — it systematically under-calls demand in the exact promotion weeks that matter most for stocking decisions, which is what drove the $2.4M in avoided stockout and expiry losses once the per-series approach replaced it. The tradeoff was real: per-series models are expensive to retrain at this scale, which is why the distributed backtesting/tuning pipeline mattered as much as the modeling choice itself.

Measurement and limitations

Impact was measured as ~$2.4M in avoided stockout and expiry losses for the client, confirmed against the incumbent (coarser) forecasting process [[TODO: exact measurement window and the accuracy metric used — MAPE/WAPE — for the interview-ready version of this number]]. Honest limitations: cold-start SKUs with little promo history are the weakest segment (the Russia engagement’s 50k-model / 84%-accuracy split specifically called out this high-quality vs. low-quality segment gap), and the per-series design is expensive enough to retrain that refresh cadence is itself a cost tradeoff, not a free dial to turn.
